Reading the exam way

Part One of the official paper is worth 12 marks out of 20. This unit trains the habits behind every one of those marks: reading in passes, decoding what a question really wants, and answering in your own words.

1Meet the paperWhere the 20 marks are, and how to spend 150 minutes

The English paper for LS and SE lasts 2 hours 30 minutes. It has two parts: Reading Comprehension (12 marks) on one selection of about 700 words, and Writing (8 marks), where you choose one of two prompts and write 250–300 words.

2Read in three passesSkim, read the questions, then scan

Expert readers do not read an exam text once from top to bottom. They read it in passes, each with a different goal:

  1. Skim the text: the title, the preview line, and the first and last sentence of each paragraph. Goal: the general idea in two minutes.
  2. Read the questions before reading in detail. Now you know what to look for.
  3. Scan: move quickly to the paragraph a question names and hunt for the key words. Only then construct your answer.

Try it. Press Skim view: everything fades except the first and last sentences of each paragraph. That is the skeleton of the text.

4Command words decodedThe first word of a question tells you the job
Command wordWhat the examiner wants
IdentifyShow who or what something is: name the main idea, a reason, a person. Short and exact.
Define, list, locateDefine: say what a term means and how it differs from similar things. List: write related points that start with the same part of speech. Locate: find where it is in the text.
Describe, explain, discuss, clarifyDescribe: give a picture in words. Explain: make clear and give reasons, especially cause and effect or steps in order.
InferDraw a meaning the writer suggests but does not state. The writer implies; you, the reader, infer and say it explicitly.
Analyse, evaluateAnalyse: examine details and the links between them to reach a conclusion. Evaluate: judge the value of something, its pluses and minuses, with evidence.

5Three depths of readingOn the lines, between the lines, beyond the lines

CRDP describes three levels of comprehension. The paper mixes all three, and the deeper ones carry the harder marks.

LevelWhat you doTypical question
Literal (on the lines)Find information that is stated: facts, sequences, stated causes, characters, setting.“Scan paragraph 3 for two indicators…”
Inferential (between the lines)Build meaning that is suggested: implied causes, the writer’s attitude, the meaning of a word from context, what a pronoun refers to.“Infer one measure that could…”
Critical (beyond the lines)Judge the text: evidence, facts versus opinions, whether an argument holds, how it connects to the wider world.“Do you agree with the writer? Justify.”

6Answer in your own wordsComplete sentences, one idea per half mark, no copying

Section A of the official paper says: Answer each of the following questions in 1–4 complete sentences using your own words. Four habits earn the marks:

  1. Echo the question in your first words, so the answer stands alone: “The first reason was…”.
  2. Count the marks. A 1-mark question usually wants two ideas (0.5 each). Number them in your head: first… second…
  3. Rephrase: change the words and the structure, keep the meaning. Copying whole lines loses marks for language.
  4. Stop when the ideas are given. Four sentences is a maximum, not a target.

Try the own-words meter. Choose a sentence from the text, rewrite it, and the meter shows how much you copied: every run of four words or more that also appears in the original is highlighted.

Allowed to keep: names, numbers and technical terms (melatonin, algorithm, insulin). You cannot paraphrase “Tripoli”, and you do not need to.
7Inferring: reading between the linesClue + what you know = inference

An inference is never a guess. It is built from a clue in the text plus common knowledge, and you should be able to point to the clue. In your answer, show the link: “Since…, we can infer that…”.

Clue (P1): “Just one more video,” he tells himself, as he did an hour ago. What can you infer about Karim?

Clue (P8): The first nights were “terrible”, but by the second week his grades had begun to rise. What does the writer want us to infer?

Clue (P1): “Shocked, she shared it at once with a warning to her friends.” What can you infer about Rana?

Clue (P8): “Today she runs a small fact-checking page.” What does this suggest?

Tap a card to flip it. Then tell the deck how well you knew it: cards you find hard come back sooner.

The three passesHow do you read an exam text?

1. Skim: title, preview, first and last sentence of each paragraph.
2. Read the questions.
3. Scan the named paragraph for the key idea, then answer.

Skim vs scanWhat is the difference?

Skim = read fast for the general idea.
Scan = search fast for specific information (a name, a number, a reason).

IdentifyWhat does this command word ask?

Show who or what something is: name the idea, reason or person exactly. Short, precise answers.

ExplainWhat does this command word ask?

Make clear and give reasons: show cause and effect, or the steps in order. Use because, so, as a result.

Imply or infer?Who does which?

The writer implies (suggests). The reader infers (works it out and states it). Infer = clue + knowledge.

EvaluateWhat does this command word ask?

Judge the value of something: give its pluses and minuses, each backed by evidence.

The three depthsName the levels of reading

Literal: on the lines (stated).
Inferential: between the lines (suggested).
Critical: beyond the lines (judged).

Usually two ideas, 0.5 each, in 1–4 complete sentences, in your own words. Echo the question at the start.

Exercise 1: Section A, exam style

4 marks, about 20 min

Answer each question in 1–4 complete sentences using your own words.

1.Based on paragraph 1, identify two signs that Karim’s night is not a healthy one.(01)
Look at the time and at what he keeps doing.
First, Karim is still awake at 1:40 a.m. although he has school the next morning. Second, he keeps promising himself to watch only one more video but does not stop.0.5 for each sign. Accept: he has a test he has not slept enough for.
2.Scan paragraphs 3 and 4 for two reasons teenagers find it hard to fall asleep early.(01)
One reason is inside the body; the other is inside the phone.
The first reason is biological: a teenager’s body produces the sleep hormone about two hours later than a child’s, and screen light delays it even more. The second is the design of apps, which keep users watching and checking because rewards come at unexpected moments.0.5 for each reason.
3.Based on paragraphs 5 and 6, explain how lack of sleep affects students’ school life.(01)
“Explain” wants cause and effect: less sleep → what happens in class, and → what happens to mood?
Because the brain stores new learning during deep sleep, tired students cannot focus in class and forget much of what they revised. In addition, they become anxious and bad-tempered, and some depend on energy drinks, which keeps them awake again the next night.0.5 for the effect on learning, 0.5 for the effect on mood or health.
4.Based on paragraphs 7 and 8, infer one measure a family and one measure a school could take to protect teenagers’ sleep.(01)
“Infer one measure” = one for the family, one for the school. Paraphrase; do not copy the “phone basket” sentence word for word.
A family could agree that all phones, including the parents’, are left outside the bedrooms at a fixed time every night. A school could hold sessions that explain how sleep affects memory, so that students choose to sleep instead of being ordered to.0.5 for each measure. Any reasonable measure linked to the paragraphs is accepted.
1.Based on paragraph 1, identify two mistakes Rana made.(01)
What did she do, and what did she not do before doing it?
First, Rana shared the photo immediately, while she was still shocked. Second, she did not check where or when the photo had been taken, so she spread a false story.0.5 for each mistake.
2.Scan paragraphs 3 and 4 for two reasons false stories spread quickly online.(01)
One reason is technical; the other is about money.
First, algorithms push alarming posts to the top because anger and fear keep users scrolling longer. Second, websites earn money from every click, so they use exaggerated headlines to attract readers.0.5 for each reason.
3.Based on paragraphs 5 and 6, explain how false news harms society.(01)
Think about trust, and about how people relate to each other.
When false reports spread, people stop trusting all news, so even honest journalists lose credibility. Moreover, since each person mostly sees opinions like his or her own, society divides into groups that no longer listen to each other.0.5 for each harm.
4.Based on paragraphs 7 and 8, infer one measure an individual and one measure an educational institution could take against false news.(01)
One measure for “me before I share”, one for a school or university.
Before sharing a post, an individual could check who published it, when, and whether trusted sources confirm it. A university could support student fact-checking groups or offer courses that train students to judge the media critically.0.5 for each measure.

Session 2 (about 2½ hours): the precise sections of Part One that students lose easy marks on.

1False statements: the six trapsHow examiners turn a true line into a false one

Section B gives sentences that are false because they misinterpret the meaning stated or conveyed in the selection. Each is a true idea with one thing changed. Find the change and you have the answer. The usual traps are:

TrapExample of the change
Antonym: a word is replaced by its oppositelater → earlier
Tense: the time is changedis falling asleep earlier → used to fall asleep earlier
General ↔ specific: “some” becomes “all”, or the reversesome families → all families; only → also
Cause ↔ effect: the two are swappedA causes B → B causes A
Wrong person: an act or opinion is given to someone elsethe researcher said → the mother said
Wrong order: the sequence of events is changedbefore → after

Trap spotter

For each false statement, choose the trap. Then compare your rewrite with the correct one.

2Rewriting a false statementFix only what is wrong

The official answer keeps the statement and corrects the changed part. Do not write “This is false because…”, and do not add a new idea. One clean, correct sentence earns the 0.5 mark.

Statement: Diabetes 1 can be cured by regular doses of insulin.
Official correction (2019): Diabetes 1 can be treated by regular doses of insulin.
Only one word changed, because only one word was wrong.

Self-check. False: All Lebanese families have introduced a phone basket. Which correction is best?

3Reference wordsWhat does each bold pronoun refer to?

Section F asks what a bold-typed word refers to. A pronoun needs an antecedent: the noun or idea it stands for, usually in the same or the previous sentence. Write the antecedent in full, as the text says it.

KindWordsWatch out
Personalhe, she, it, they, him, her, them, his, its, their…it can refer to a whole idea, not only a noun.
Relativewho, whom, whose, which, that, where, whenThe antecedent is usually just before it: the room where…
Demonstrativethis, that, these, thosethis often sums up the previous sentence.
Indefiniteone, each, some, both, many, several, none…Check singular or plural.

5The fill-in taskGrammar first, meaning second, spelling always

Section E gives a box of words underlined in the text and sentences with blanks. A fast method:

  1. Grammar: what does the blank need? After the → a noun; after very → an adjective; after to → a verb.
  2. Meaning: reread the word in its paragraph, then test it in the sentence.
  3. Spelling: the official grading scheme says the spelling must be correct. Copy the word letter by letter.

6Putting Part One togetherA route through the 12 marks

On exam day, a reliable route through Part One looks like this. Adapt the minutes to the plan you chose in Lesson 1.

  1. Skim the text and the preview (5 min).
  2. Read all the questions and underline the command words and paragraph numbers (5 min).
  3. Do the quick-win sections first: false statements, reference words and vocabulary (15 min).
  4. Then the written answers of Sections A and C, scanning paragraph by paragraph (35 min).
  5. The table or graph question last if it is long (10 min), then check spelling.

Then try the timed mini-paper in Practice 2, Exercise 3.
