The Price of a Click

Original practice text written for this platform. Names, places and figures are invented for practice.

On a rainy evening in Tripoli, Rana, a nineteen-year-old business student, saw a dramatic photo of a collapsed bridge on her phone. Shocked, she shared it at once with a warning to her friends. Within an hour, the post had been shared more than 4,000 times. There was only one problem: the bridge was standing, and the photo had been taken in another country six years earlier.

Rana’s mistake is far from rare. In a recent survey of 900 young adults in Lebanon, three out of four said that social media is their main source of news. Fewer than one in five said they check where a story comes from before passing it on, so false stories circulate almost as freely as true ones.

Part of the problem lies in how social platforms choose what we see. Their programs, called algorithms, are designed to show each user the content which is most likely to keep him or her scrolling. Since anger and fear hold our attention longer than calm facts, the most alarming posts are pushed to the top, whether they are true or not.

Money also plays a role. Every click brings advertising income, so some websites compete with sensational headlines that promise more than the article delivers. “A headline used to summarise a story; now it often sells it,” says Ziad Khoury, a journalism lecturer. “When attention becomes the product, accuracy becomes optional.”

The consequences reach far beyond one embarrassing post. When false reports spread, people begin to doubt all news, including the reliable kind, and the credibility of serious journalists suffers. Moreover, since each user mostly sees opinions similar to his or her own, communities split into separate groups that no longer listen to one another.

Ironically, this generation has more information at its fingertips than any before it, yet it may be less informed. Endless headlines create the feeling of knowing what is happening, while the full story stays unread.

The solution is not to switch off the media but to read it more wisely. Before sharing, readers can ask three quick questions: who published this, when, and do other trusted sources report it too? A reverse image search takes less than a minute and can verify whether a photo is new. Universities and schools can also teach media literacy as seriously as they teach mathematics.

As for Rana, she learned her lesson. Today she runs a small fact-checking page with three classmates on their campus, where students send in doubtful posts to be checked before they spread.

The media can be a window on the world or a mirror that only shows us what we want to see. Which one it becomes depends, in the end, on the reader’s finger: the one that pauses before it clicks.

A. Answer each of the following questions in 1–4 complete sentences using your own words.

1.Based on paragraph 1, identify two mistakes Rana made.(01)
First, Rana shared the photo immediately, while she was still shocked. Second, she did not check where or when the photo had been taken, so she spread a false story.0.5 for each mistake.
2.Scan paragraphs 3 and 4 for two reasons false stories spread quickly online.(01)
First, algorithms push alarming posts to the top because anger and fear keep users scrolling longer. Second, websites earn money from every click, so they use exaggerated headlines to attract readers.0.5 for each reason.
3.Based on paragraphs 5 and 6, explain how false news harms society.(01)
When false reports spread, people stop trusting all news, so even honest journalists lose credibility. Moreover, since each person mostly sees opinions like his or her own, society divides into groups that no longer listen to each other.0.5 for each harm.
4.Based on paragraphs 7 and 8, infer one measure an individual and one measure an educational institution could take against false news.(01)
Before sharing a post, an individual could check who published it, when, and whether trusted sources confirm it. A university could support student fact-checking groups or offer courses that train students to judge the media critically.0.5 for each measure.
