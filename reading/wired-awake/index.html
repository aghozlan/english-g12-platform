Wired Awake

Original practice text written for this platform. Names, places and figures are invented for practice.

It is 1:40 a.m. in a quiet apartment in Saida, and Karim, seventeen, is still awake. His lights are off, but his face glows blue above the pillow. “Just one more video,” he tells himself, as he did an hour ago. At seven, his alarm will ring for a physics test that he has not slept enough to remember. Karim is not lazy, and he is not alone: he belongs to a generation whose nights have quietly been taken over by a screen.

Sleep scientists agree that teenagers need between eight and ten hours of sleep a night. Yet in a recent survey of 1,200 secondary students in Beirut and the South, only one in five reported sleeping eight hours on school nights. More than 70 percent said that their phone was the last thing they looked at before sleeping and the first thing they checked on waking.

The problem is partly biological. During adolescence, the body releases melatonin, the hormone that makes us sleepy, about two hours later than it does in childhood. A teenager’s brain is therefore naturally ready to sleep later. The bright blue light of a screen delays this hormone even further, so it tricks the brain into believing that it is still daytime.

However, biology is only half the story. Many apps are designed to keep users scrolling. “Videos play automatically one after another, and notifications arrive at unpredictable times,” explains Dr Maya Haddad, a sleep researcher. “It works like a slot machine: you never know when the next reward will come, so you keep checking.” Each like or message gives a small burst of pleasure, so putting the phone down starts to feel like a loss.

The effects appear in the classroom the next morning. Tired students find it harder to concentrate, and their memory suffers, since the brain uses deep sleep to store what was learned during the day. Teachers notice heads on desks in the first period. Ironically, many students stay up late to study for the next day’s test, only to forget much of what they reviewed.

Lack of sleep also affects mood and health. Teenagers who sleep less than seven hours are more likely to feel anxious, irritable and sad. Some reach for energy drinks to survive the school day, which makes it harder to fall asleep the following night. The result is a cycle that is hard to break.

Fortunately, small changes can make a large difference. Experts recommend switching off screens an hour before bed and charging the phone outside the bedroom, where it cannot call for attention. Some families have introduced a “phone basket” in the living room at ten o’clock, a rule that parents follow too. Schools can help as well, by teaching students how sleep works rather than simply telling them to sleep more.

Karim tried the basket for a month. “The first three nights were terrible,” he admits. “I kept thinking I was missing something.” By the second week, he was falling asleep before midnight, and his grades had begun to rise.

Technology is not the enemy: it helps teenagers learn, create and stay connected. Yet a phone should be a tool that we use, not a master that decides when we sleep. If we want a generation that is truly awake during the day, we must first help it rest at night.

A. Answer each of the following questions in 1–4 complete sentences using your own words.

1.Based on paragraph 1, identify two signs that Karim’s night is not a healthy one.(01)
First, Karim is still awake at 1:40 a.m. although he has school the next morning. Second, he keeps promising himself to watch only one more video but does not stop.0.5 for each sign. Accept: he has a test he has not slept enough for.
2.Scan paragraphs 3 and 4 for two reasons teenagers find it hard to fall asleep early.(01)
The first reason is biological: a teenager’s body produces the sleep hormone about two hours later than a child’s, and screen light delays it even more. The second is the design of apps, which keep users watching and checking because rewards come at unexpected moments.0.5 for each reason.
3.Based on paragraphs 5 and 6, explain how lack of sleep affects students’ school life.(01)
Because the brain stores new learning during deep sleep, tired students cannot focus in class and forget much of what they revised. In addition, they become anxious and bad-tempered, and some depend on energy drinks, which keeps them awake again the next night.0.5 for the effect on learning, 0.5 for the effect on mood or health.
4.Based on paragraphs 7 and 8, infer one measure a family and one measure a school could take to protect teenagers’ sleep.(01)
A family could agree that all phones, including the parents’, are left outside the bedrooms at a fixed time every night. A school could hold sessions that explain how sleep affects memory, so that students choose to sleep instead of being ordered to.0.5 for each measure. Any reasonable measure linked to the paragraphs is accepted.
