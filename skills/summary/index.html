Summarising and paraphrasing

A paraphrase repeats almost everything in new words; a summary keeps only the main points. Both are written in your own words.

On the paperReal questions

Summarise paragraph … in two or three sentences of your own.Typical form, from the course notes

How it’s markedThe official split

A summary loses marks for copied sentences and for minor details. It earns them for the main idea and the key supporting points, in your own words.

The methodHow to answer

A paragraph: ask who, what, when, where, why and how, then combine the answers.

  1. Skim: introduction and thesis, the topic sentence and concluding sentence of each body paragraph, the conclusion.
  2. Read in detail and take short notes.
  3. Combine the notes in your own words into one paragraph.
  4. Start with the title and the writer: “In Wired Awake, the writer explains…”

See it doneWorked answers

Question. Summarise paragraph 3 of Wired Awake in one or two sentences.
The writer explains that teenagers sleep late partly for biological reasons: their bodies release the sleep hormone two hours later than children’s, and screen light delays it further.

The main point and its two supports, in new words, no examples.

Question. Summarise paragraph 4 of The Price of a Click in one or two sentences.
Because websites earn money from every click, some of them use exaggerated headlines to attract readers, putting attention before accuracy.

Cause, effect and the key idea, without the quotation.

Texts: Wired Awake (LS) and The Price of a Click (SE), in the reading room.
