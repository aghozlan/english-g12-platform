Skim, read the questions, scan

Strong readers never read an exam text once from top to bottom. They read it in three passes, each with its own job.

On the paperThe preview line is your first clue

The methodThree passes

  1. Skim: the preview, the title, then the first and last sentence of each paragraph. In two minutes you have the skeleton of the text.
  2. Read the questions, all of them, before reading in detail. Now you know what to look for, and which paragraphs matter.
  3. Scan: go to the paragraph a question names and hunt for its key idea. Only then write.

In the following selection, the writer explores how smartphones keep teenagers awake at night and what this does to their learning and health. Read it carefully, and then answer the questions that follow.

Wired Awake

Original practice text written for this platform. Names, places and figures are invented for practice.

It is 1:40 a.m. in a quiet apartment in Saida, and Karim, seventeen, is still awake. His lights are off, but his face glows blue above the pillow. “Just one more video,” he tells himself, as he did an hour ago. At seven, his alarm will ring for a physics test that he has not slept enough to remember. Karim is not lazy, and he is not alone: he belongs to a generation whose nights have quietly been taken over by a screen.

Sleep scientists agree that teenagers need between eight and ten hours of sleep a night. Yet in a recent survey of 1,200 secondary students in Beirut and the South, only one in five reported sleeping eight hours on school nights. More than 70 percent said that their phone was the last thing they looked at before sleeping and the first thing they checked on waking.

The problem is partly biological. During adolescence, the body releases melatonin, the hormone that makes us sleepy, about two hours later than it does in childhood. A teenager’s brain is therefore naturally ready to sleep later. The bright blue light of a screen delays this hormone even further, so it tricks the brain into believing that it is still daytime.

However, biology is only half the story. Many apps are designed to keep users scrolling. “Videos play automatically one after another, and notifications arrive at unpredictable times,” explains Dr Maya Haddad, a sleep researcher. “It works like a slot machine: you never know when the next reward will come, so you keep checking.” Each like or message gives a small burst of pleasure, so putting the phone down starts to feel like a loss.

The effects appear in the classroom the next morning. Tired students find it harder to concentrate, and their memory suffers, since the brain uses deep sleep to store what was learned during the day. Teachers notice heads on desks in the first period. Ironically, many students stay up late to study for the next day’s test, only to forget much of what they reviewed.

Lack of sleep also affects mood and health. Teenagers who sleep less than seven hours are more likely to feel anxious, irritable and sad. Some reach for energy drinks to survive the school day, which makes it harder to fall asleep the following night. The result is a cycle that is hard to break.

Fortunately, small changes can make a large difference. Experts recommend switching off screens an hour before bed and charging the phone outside the bedroom, where it cannot call for attention. Some families have introduced a “phone basket” in the living room at ten o’clock, a rule that parents follow too. Schools can help as well, by teaching students how sleep works rather than simply telling them to sleep more.

Karim tried the basket for a month. “The first three nights were terrible,” he admits. “I kept thinking I was missing something.” By the second week, he was falling asleep before midnight, and his grades had begun to rise.

Technology is not the enemy: it helps teenagers learn, create and stay connected. Yet a phone should be a tool that we use, not a master that decides when we sleep. If we want a generation that is truly awake during the day, we must first help it rest at night.

In the following selection, the writer examines how social media has changed the way young people receive the news, and what it costs society. Read it carefully, and then answer the questions that follow.

The Price of a Click

Original practice text written for this platform. Names, places and figures are invented for practice.

On a rainy evening in Tripoli, Rana, a nineteen-year-old business student, saw a dramatic photo of a collapsed bridge on her phone. Shocked, she shared it at once with a warning to her friends. Within an hour, the post had been shared more than 4,000 times. There was only one problem: the bridge was standing, and the photo had been taken in another country six years earlier.

Rana’s mistake is far from rare. In a recent survey of 900 young adults in Lebanon, three out of four said that social media is their main source of news. Fewer than one in five said they check where a story comes from before passing it on, so false stories circulate almost as freely as true ones.

Part of the problem lies in how social platforms choose what we see. Their programs, called algorithms, are designed to show each user the content which is most likely to keep him or her scrolling. Since anger and fear hold our attention longer than calm facts, the most alarming posts are pushed to the top, whether they are true or not.

Money also plays a role. Every click brings advertising income, so some websites compete with sensational headlines that promise more than the article delivers. “A headline used to summarise a story; now it often sells it,” says Ziad Khoury, a journalism lecturer. “When attention becomes the product, accuracy becomes optional.”

The consequences reach far beyond one embarrassing post. When false reports spread, people begin to doubt all news, including the reliable kind, and the credibility of serious journalists suffers. Moreover, since each user mostly sees opinions similar to his or her own, communities split into separate groups that no longer listen to one another.

Ironically, this generation has more information at its fingertips than any before it, yet it may be less informed. Endless headlines create the feeling of knowing what is happening, while the full story stays unread.

The solution is not to switch off the media but to read it more wisely. Before sharing, readers can ask three quick questions: who published this, when, and do other trusted sources report it too? A reverse image search takes less than a minute and can verify whether a photo is new. Universities and schools can also teach media literacy as seriously as they teach mathematics.

As for Rana, she learned her lesson. Today she runs a small fact-checking page with three classmates on their campus, where students send in doubtful posts to be checked before they spread.

The media can be a window on the world or a mirror that only shows us what we want to see. Which one it becomes depends, in the end, on the reader’s finger: the one that pauses before it clicks.
